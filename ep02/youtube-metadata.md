## Description

The sky is blue at midday and red at sunset, and both colours come from the same sunlight and the same air. The only thing that changes is how much air the light has to cross.

In this video we follow sunlight into the atmosphere and watch what air molecules do to each colour. Blue light is scattered about six times more strongly than red, so the sky fills with blue. At sunset the light crosses about 38 times more air, the blue is scattered out along the way, and what reaches you is orange and red. That missing blue is not lost: it is lighting the afternoon sky for people to the west of you.

What you'll see
• Why air scatters short wavelengths far more than long ones (the 1/λ⁴ rule)
• Why you see blue when you look away from the sun, and an almost white sun when you look at it
• Why violet light, scattered even more than blue, does not win
• Why a sunset is the light that is left after about 38 atmospheres of air
• Why clouds are white, and why Mars has an orange day and a blue sunset

How this video was made
Script, narration and animation were produced with AI tools. The narration uses a synthetic voice. The animations are computer simulations, not footage. The photon counts and the colours of the setting sun are calculated from the published Rayleigh optical depth of the atmosphere (0.221 at 450 nm, 0.036 at 700 nm) and a horizon air mass of 38, for clean air. The "why not violet" scene is a diagram: the size of each of the three effects is illustrative.

# Ep 58 claims (stopped-clock illusion / chronostasis)
| Claim | Source |
| --- | --- |
| After moving your eyes to a ticking clock, the first tick can seem to last longer (stopped-clock illusion) | Yarrow, Haggard, Heal, Brown & Rothwell 2001, Nature 414:302 (City Research Online abstract); Wikipedia "Chronostasis"; Scientific American "Research Reveals Why Clocks Sometimes Seem to Stop" |
| It is not the clock or the video: the effect comes from the eye movement (saccade) | Wikipedia (moving attention without moving the eyes does not trigger it); Yarrow 2001 |
| Vision is suppressed during the saccade (saccadic masking) | Wikipedia "Chronostasis" |
| The brain fills the gap with the new image, extended back to about the start of the eye movement | Yarrow 2001 abstract ("extends the percept of the saccadic target backwards in time to just before the onset of the saccade"); Wikipedia (neural antedating) |
| The first interval can feel up to about half a second longer | Wikipedia "Chronostasis" ("by up to half a second") |
| Bigger eye jumps give a bigger effect | Wikipedia; Scientific American |
| People make about 3 saccades a second | MIT Schiller lab, "The Neural Control of Visually Guided Eye Movements" ("about 3 saccadic eye movements a second") |
Notes: on a phone screen the eye jump is small, so the effect is weaker than with a wall clock across a room; the narration asks "Did your clock freeze?" and never promises it will, and the description suggests trying a real clock. The on-screen second hand ticks exactly once per second of video time. Pictures are schematic.

# Ep 61 claims (Aristotle's illusion: crossed fingers, two noses)
| Claim | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Cross two fingers and touch one small round object (a nose, a pea, a pen) between the tips: it can feel like two objects | Popular Science "Three ancient optical illusions explained by modern science" (nose or pen, eyes shut); Illusions Index "Aristotle's Illusion"; Hanover ISLE "Aristotle's illusion" |
| It is not the fingers: the skin reports correctly, the brain misreads it ("Your brain still thinks they're uncrossed") | Illusions Index (brain "assumes" the usual, uncrossed situation); Hanover ISLE (attributed to how the nervous system is organised) |
| Crossed, the object touches the outer sides of the two fingers; in the normal hand those sides face away from each other and are usually touched by two different objects | Hanover ISLE ("When you crossed your fingers, you are touching the outsides of each finger. Usually when they are touched, it is by two different objects."); Illusions Index |
| Aristotle wrote about it more than two thousand years ago (Aristotle lived 384–322 BC; the on-screen label "2,000+ years" matches the narration) | Aristotle, On Dreams (Parva Naturalia, 460b): crossed fingers make one object feel like two (MIT Internet Classics Archive); Illusions Index; Hanover ISLE |
| Lab support: one stimulus on two displaced fingertips was felt as two with probability 0.96 ("tactile diplopia") | Benedetti 1986, PubMed 3774481 (description only, not voiced) |
Hedges: the narration says "feels like two?" and ends on "Two?", so it never promises every viewer feels it (the description says many people do, and that it is clearer with eyes shut). The ghost "brain map" fingers and pink noses are schematic, not a model of the cortex.
